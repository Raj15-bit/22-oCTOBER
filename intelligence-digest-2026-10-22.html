01

The big picture

Dearer money, dearer oil and forced selling all hit Indian shares at once, while the AI boom abroad showed its first real money strain.

In short
  • India's interest rates and oil costs both went up, and that hurt smaller Indian shares the most.
  • Foreign funds sold Indian shares and placed large bets on a further fall, while local funds kept buying.
  • Abroad, the companies spending most on AI now need to borrow heavily, and lenders are getting nervous.

The mood in Indian markets turned dark even though the economy itself is not weak. Factories, cars and festive shopping are doing well. The trouble is the price of money and the price of oil, and both are set by forces far from India.

3 pressures meet in India

The first pressure is rates. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI, the central bank) raised its main lending rate in October and hinted at more, because prices are rising faster. The second is oil, now above $110 a barrel after attacks in Saudi Arabia. The third is forced selling: people who bought shares with borrowed money got margin calls (demands to put in more cash or sell).

The oil bill is the link

India buys most of its oil from abroad, so a dearer barrel quickly becomes a bigger import bill. In September India paid 47% more for crude than a year earlier, though it bought only 7% more oil by weight.

Reading between the lines, the oil bill explains much of the rate story. More dollars leave India to pay for oil, which pushes up prices at home and puts pressure on the rupee. The RBI then has to keep rates high and spend money to hold the rupee steady. So the war in the Gulf reaches Indian borrowers through their loan rates, not only through petrol prices.

The AI boom is now a money question

In the US, the AI story changed from "who wins" to "who pays". Meta spent more on data centres than it earned in cash, and its shares fell about 6% in a day. Bonds of AI cloud firms such as CoreWeave now pay junk-level interest. Section 09 has the details.

This matters for India in a quiet way. When AI firms borrow hundreds of billions, they compete with governments for the same savings, which keeps world interest rates high. High US rates make Indian shares less attractive to foreign funds. So the AI boom abroad and the rate pain at home are part of the same chain.

Views split on the bottom

One view is that the fall is close to its end: good results are being sold anyway, local funds keep buying, and a very gloomy newspaper headline is often a sign that most sellers are done. The opposite view is that foreign funds hold near-record bets on a fall, oil and rates are still rising, and forced selling has not finished. Section 10 sets both sides out in a table.

02

The price of money in India

The RBI has started raising rates again, bond lenders want even more, and the rupee's strength is being bought at a cost.

In short
  • The RBI raised its main rate to 5.50% and signalled that more rises may come.
  • Lenders to the government now ask for the highest interest in about 4 years.
  • The rupee got stronger, but partly because the RBI has promised to sell a very large amount of dollars later.

Rates are going up, not down

In October the RBI raised the repo rate (the rate at which it lends to banks) to 5.50%, its first rise since February 2023. Its rate committee also signalled further rises, because retail inflation hit a 23-month high in September.

  • Food — retail inflation was 5.54% in September and food prices rose 7.74% from a year earlier, driven by a weak monsoon and costly fuel.
  • Bajaj Finance — told investors it expects 2 more rate rises this year, taking the repo rate to about 6%.
  • Banks — ICICI Bank said its lending margin should recover from the March quarter, "assuming no further rate hikes"; HDFC Bank said its margin is "at the bottom".
  • J&K Bank — its net interest margin fell to 3.17% from 3.56%, as deposits got dearer faster than loans.

Bond lenders want more

A bond yield is the yearly interest a lender demands to part with money for that long. Yields on Indian government bonds rose across every length of loan. The 30-year yield matched its June 2022 high.

A forward sale is a promise to sell dollars on a future date. The RBI uses it to hold the rupee up without spending its reserves today. One view is that this book has grown too large and the RBI should let the rupee find its own level. The opposite view is that the RBI's steps are working: the rupee is rising on its own, and a stronger rupee cuts the oil bill, which helps bring inflation down.

With rates rising, lenders are moving to safer loans backed by gold, shares or homes. Bajaj Finance, the largest non-bank lender, shows this clearly. Its fastest-growing loans are the ones that earn the least interest.

Gold loans carry their own risk. A study of last year's lending found that 15% to 23% of new gold loans went to people already more than 90 days late on other loans. Reading between the lines, some borrowers are pawning gold to keep up with other debts, which is a sign of strain under the good spending numbers.

  • Gold loan size — gold loans outstanding reached ₹21.7 lakh crore; jewellery loans at non-banks grew 69.1% and at banks 83.2% in a year.
  • Takeover loans — since the RBI let banks fund company takeovers from 1 July, they have lent over ₹10,000 crore across about a dozen deals, up to 75% of the deal value.
  • Examples — Crystal Crop Protection buying FMC's India business, Waaree Renewable Technologies buying a stake in Associated Power, and a ChrysCap-led group buying into Novartis India.
  • Insurers — Bajaj Life and Bajaj General raised money in private credit funds from ₹200 crore to ₹650 crore, and insurers want the 3% limit raised to 5%.

Why foreign money stays away

Neelkanth Mishra of the World Bank argued that cutting taxes will not bring foreign investors back, because US bonds now pay well and India's rules are complex. That fits the numbers in section 04, where foreign funds keep selling. Separately, SEBI will let portfolio managers invest directly abroad from April 2027, which could send some Indian money the other way.

So the price of money is rising from 3 sides at once: the RBI, the bond market and the world. Until oil and inflation cool, the cheapest loans of the past 2 years are not coming back.

03

Oil, ships and war

The fighting moved onto Saudi soil, the real price of oil pulled away from the screen price, and America's emergency oil store fell to a 44-year low.

In short
  • Drones hit a Saudi oil field and airport, and oil rose to its highest price since May.
  • Buyers of real cargoes pay far more than the price on screens, mostly because shipping and insurance got very dear.
  • Refiners earn record profits, while airlines and farmers pay the bill.

The war reaches Saudi oil

Houthi drones (from the Iran-backed group in Yemen) hit Saudi Aramco's Shaybah oil field and the airport at Dammam. Brent crude, the world's main oil price, rose as much as 3.3% to $112.43 on 22 October. Reports differ on how many died, and an Indian was among those killed at Dammam.

  • Aramco — told staff at its Dhahran head office to leave and work from home; it says output has not changed. The Shaybah field can pump about 1 million barrels a day.
  • US staff — the US ordered some embassy staff and families to leave Saudi Arabia.
  • Tankers — the UK's shipping-safety office counts a record of more than 2 dozen attacks on ships in October. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed 7 tankers hit near Hormuz in 24 hours, which could not be confirmed.
  • Indian crew — the Kuwait-flagged tanker AL DASMA, with 21 Indians on board, was hit by a projectile near Hormuz; the fire was put out and all are safe.

2 prices for the same oil

The price on trading screens is for oil to be delivered later. Refiners who need a cargo now pay the "Dated Brent" price, and on 21 October it was about $20 higher. The gap is mostly the cost of moving oil safely.

Goldman Sachs expects most of the shipping cost to stay as long as the war lasts. War-risk insurance has risen many times over, and a tanker without cover does not sail. A Bloomberg column argued that oil can now cost $70 or $150 a barrel depending on where the buyer stands. The near-month Brent price is about $4 above the next month, a sign that oil is short right now.

One view is that the calm screen price is misleading and the physical market is very tight. The opposite view points to flows: oil leaving the Middle East was above 24 million barrels a day, more than before the war, and Hormuz is carrying about 82% of its old volume. A price chart also shows Brent stalling near its old peak with weakening momentum. Both can be true: plenty of oil moves, but at a far higher delivered cost.

What may happen after the US vote

The New York Times reported that the US and Israel drafted plans for about 3 days of strikes on Iran after the US midterm elections on 3 November. President Trump said he is not negotiating with Iran and accused it of "making up fake" attacks to lift oil prices before the vote.

  • Iran's oil — its stored oil at sea near Asia was fully used up by mid-October, according to Kpler, because of the US naval blockade.
  • Iran's words — Foreign Minister Araghchi wrote of a "just and honourable deal"; President Pezeshkian said sanctions must go first.
  • Allies — UK, French, Pakistani and Egyptian forces are now helping defend Saudi Arabia, and a US envoy told Gulf allies the US cannot guarantee their security.

One view is that the present pause is a staging period, with Israel's election and the US vote both close, and that escalation follows. The opposite view reads Iran's offer of talks as a real opening. Reading between the lines, oil traders cannot price a 3-day bombing campaign in advance, which is part of why the physical premium is so large.

The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve, its emergency oil store, fell to 282.7 million barrels, the lowest since 1982. Counting crude, petrol, diesel and jet fuel together, US stocks are the lowest since at least 1990.

  • Airlines — domestic passengers fell 6.5% in September to 118.2 lakh, the lowest since November 2022; jet fuel costs about ₹137 a litre.
  • Airline losses — rating agency ICRA expects the industry to lose ₹36,000 to ₹38,000 crore this year; SpiceJet carried only 73,000 people in September.

04

India's share market

Forced selling hit small shares, foreign funds bet heavily on a fall, and only IT shares and big share sales drew strong buying.

In short
  • Most Indian shares are in a deep fall even though the Nifty 50 looks fairly calm.
  • People who bought with borrowed money are being forced to sell, which pulls good companies down too.
  • IT shares had their best month in years, and the Jio share sale was bought many times over.

A rout under a calm index

Listed Indian companies lost about ₹6 lakh crore of value on 21 October alone. Over 2 sessions the loss equalled the whole value of HDFC Bank. On 22 October the Nifty closed at 22,688.

  • Breadth — only 29.9% of NSE stocks trade above their 200-day average (a common test of a long-term uptrend), back to the May level.
  • Bear markets — 56% of Nifty 50 companies are in a bear market of their own, meaning 20% or more below their peak.
  • New lows — about 24% of stocks are at 52-week lows, while new highs are close to zero.

Selling with borrowed money

Many traders use the margin trading facility (MTF), where a broker lends money to buy shares. When prices fall, brokers ask for more cash, and traders who cannot pay must sell. In October that turned into a chain of forced selling.

  • Closing positions — in October's first 12 trading days, ₹45,840 crore of margin positions were closed against ₹43,270 crore opened.
  • First outflow — margin lending shrank by about ₹2,570 crore in October, the first monthly fall since March.
  • Morepen — Morepen Laboratories fell about 18% in 4 sessions after several brokers stopped funding it.
  • Big names too — margin loans against Infosys fell about 31%, TCS 34% and Wipro 30%.
  • Stuck shares — many small stocks sat at their lower circuit (the daily fall limit) on thin volume, so sellers could not get out.

Reading between the lines, this is why strong results did not help. Traders sell what still has a buyer, which is often the best company in their account. That also explains the sharp gap between the Nifty and small shares in section 01.

Who is buying, who is selling

Foreign institutional investors (FIIs) and domestic institutions (DIIs, mostly mutual funds) both bought on 21 October, yet prices fell. On 22 October foreign funds turned big sellers.

India's economy and spending

Factories ran at their fastest pace in 30 months and festive buying was strong, but drought, food prices and job cuts are building quietly underneath.

In short
  • Output from India's core industries grew at its fastest pace in 30 months.
  • Festive car and online sales were strong, especially in smaller towns.
  • Drought, dear food and job cuts at some tech firms are the weak spots to watch.

Factories, power and China's curbs

When digital trust breaks

An Indian certificate company lost its global website business in days, and AI tools are now finding and using software flaws faster than firms can fix them.

In short
  • eMudhra issued 8 website-security certificates with weak keys, was blocked by the big browsers, and is leaving that business.
  • An AI model found 2 serious flaws in PHP, the language behind most websites, and attackers moved within hours.
  • Indian banks and companies are exposed, and many spend too little on security.

eMudhra: a small business with a big trust problem

A certificate authority issues digital certificates that prove a website or a piece of software is real. Each certificate rests on a secret key that must be very long. Researchers found that 8 certificates from eMudhra, for websites and for signing code, had weak keys, and Mozilla said 1 key was simply the number 4.

Share price before~₹650Before the news spread
Share price after~₹350Within days; 1 day ended 20% down at ₹432
Business affected~1%Website certificates, as a share of revenue, says the company
  • Blocked — Apple, Google, Microsoft and Mozilla stopped trusting the affected certificates.
  • Exit — eMudhra's board decided on 22 October to leave the global website certificate business.
  • Government — the IT ministry (MeitY) said the impact in India is "very limited" and warned against fear-mongering; Indian digital signatures and eSign are not affected; CERT-In issued a warning.
  • Company — managers called it a "script error" and a bad day.

One view is that the fall is an overreaction, since the business lost is about 1% of sales. The opposite view is that trust is the whole product for a certificate company, so a small failure can hurt the larger business too. Reading between the lines, a security firm with weak keys invites the question of what else was checked poorly, and that doubt is hard to remove quickly.

AI is now finding the holes

PHP is the programming language behind about 70% of websites. An OpenAI model found 2 critical flaws in it, rated near the top of the danger scale, as part of a coordinated fixing effort. The fixes came out on 21 October, and attacks started within hours.

  • India — CERT-In issued a critical warning; many Indian news sites and payment apps run on PHP.
  • Japan — Prime Minister Takaichi urged firms on social media to patch.

Japan shows what a wave looks like. At least 18 Japanese companies reported breaches from 25 September, and Japan's cyber response team confirmed that attackers used AI to scan for weak points.
